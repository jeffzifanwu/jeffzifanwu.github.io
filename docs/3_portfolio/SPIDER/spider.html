1 Introduction

Consider a government designing a heating subsidy programme to reduce hospitalisations due to cold weather. One policy option is a 10% universal subsidy to all households throughout winter. Another is a 25% subsidy restricted to low-income households on days when the temperature falls below a specified threshold. Choosing between them requires knowing the effect of each policy on the policy goal, but these effects are unknown before either policy is implemented. Neither the outcomes under each policy nor what would have happened in its absence – at any future date – are directly observable at the design stage.

Policy design in practice is considerably more complex than the simple example above. The space of candidate policies is large: instrument types and intensities can vary (i.e., cash transfers vs. discounts on energy bills) and eligibility criteria can be fine-tuned (i.e., income and temperature thresholds). The objectives are multidimensional in two senses: (1) policymakers typically care about several outcomes simultaneously (e.g., reducing hospitalisations in the most equitable manner at the lowest fiscal cost and minimising adverse effects such as a surge in carbon emissions), and (2) they care not only about average outcomes but also about their distribution, penalising policies that risk catastrophic results even if they perform well on average (Manski, 2004; Ben-Tal et al., 2009). The complexity of this choice space has pushed the field toward microsimulation and stochastic optimisation methods to systematically evaluate and compare candidate policies (Bourguignon & Spadaro, 2006; Kitagawa & Tetenov, 2018).

This complexity raises the bar on the information that policy design requires. When the policy is evaluated at the unit-time level and the criterion involves risk-sensitive metrics, knowing a single population-level effect is insufficient. The required object is the distribution of policy effects (or its distributional parameters such as the mean and variance) at the level of unit-time coordinates, e.g., the distribution of the reduction in hospitalisations for a specific household in a specific year under the 10% universal subsidy.

Obtaining this object requires a causal approach rather than a predictive one. Classical statistical models and modern machine learning methods are designed to predict what will happen, i.e., whether a unit will receive treatment and what the outcome will be. But policy design asks a different question: what would happen if we were to intervene? That is, what the outcome would be if units that would not naturally receive treatment were to receive it. To distinguish these two strands, the literature has adopted the terms “predictive” and “causal” research, respectively1. Under very specific conditions, the answers to these two questions coincide. But more often, answering causal questions with predictive methods incurs the well-known selection bias problem: units that naturally receive treatment differ systematically from those that do not, and predictive models do not separate the effect of treatment from the effect of selection into treatment (J. Heckman 1979).

Two major frameworks of causal research have emerged in the social sciences. The first, which we broadly term the structural modelling framework, subsumes the structural causal models developed by computer scientists (Pearl 2009) and structural econometric models developed by economists (J. J. Heckman and Vytlacil 2007). The defining feature of this framework is that it requires researchers to specify a theory of the causal relationships among variables. Structural causal models encode this theory in a directed acyclic graph, whereas structural econometric models encode it in a behavioural model (e.g., utility maximisation). The key difference is that structural causal models are nonparametric whereas structural econometric models typically impose functional forms and distributional assumptions (i.e., specifying, not only what causes what, but how it happens). In our view, despite these technical differences, the two traditions are more alike than different when compared to the framework discussed next.

The second framework is the potential outcomes framework (Rubin1974?; Neyman1923?). This framework is agnostic about the underlying causal relationships among variables and instead focuses on the contrast between potential outcomes under different treatment statuses — for instance, the hospitalisation rate under the heating subsidy versus the hospitalisation rate in its absence. This focus gives the framework a practical advantage over the structural modelling framework: it speaks directly to the policy question of interest. Rather than first translating the policy question into a directed acyclic graph or behavioural model, the potential outcomes framework allows the treatment and outcome variables to emerge naturally from the programme being evaluated. This feature has made the potential outcomes framework the dominant language in applied social science and biomedical research (AngristPischke2009?; ImbensRubin2015?; Imbens2020?).

The potential outcomes framework is, however, oriented towards retrospective programme evaluation rather than prospective policy design. Its canonical estimands are: the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) characterises the treatment effect on the entire population; the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT) characterises the treatment effect on the historically treated sub-population; and the Average Treatment Effect on the Control (ATC) characterises the treatment effect on the historically untreated sub-population. None of these correspond to the distribution of treatment effects at the unit-time coordinates that a prospective policy, with its own assignment mechanism, would target. A stream of research on external validity addresses a related but distinct problem: how to reweight canonical estimands when the study sample is unrepresentative of the target population (Lesko2017?; DegtiarRose2020?). These methods adjust for discrepancies in subgroup composition between the sample and population, rather than discrepancies between assignment mechanisms. The resulting reweighted estimands remain aggregates over the population rather than the distribution of treatment effects at the unit-time coordinates. The gap therefore remains: the potential outcomes framework, even with external validity corrections, is unfit for contemporary policy design.

To fill this gap, we propose a prospective extension of the potential outcomes framework, which we call SPIDER: Super-population, Policy-induced parameters, Identification, Data, Estimation, and infeRence. The point of departure is a conceptualisation of all unit characteristics (e.g., household income), time characteristics (e.g., daily temperature), and potential outcomes (e.g., hospitalisation rates under the heating subsidy and in its absence) as generated by a stochastic process, which we call the super-population. At each unit-time coordinate, all characteristics are in principle observable, but only one potential outcome is observed — the one corresponding to the treatment received. Under this conceptualisation, the super-population describes the state of nature and is stable, whereas the treatment assignment mechanism is a variable that policymakers can manipulate.

SPIDER formalises the treatment assignment mechanism through a policy function that maps the characteristics of a unit-time coordinate to the probability of receiving treatment. This subsumes deterministic assignment rules as a special case, e.g., the probability of receiving the heating subsidy is one if the temperature falls below a specified threshold and zero otherwise. By integrating the super-population and the policy function, SPIDER constructs a policy-induced target measure: the joint distribution of unit characteristics, time characteristics, and treatment effects at each unit-time coordinate under the assignment mechanism specified by the policy function. This object is the foundation from which all causal parameters in SPIDER are derived.

SPIDER summarises the policy-induced target measure through two causal parameters that suffice for most policy design purposes. First, the Target Average Treatment Effect (TATE) is the mean treatment effect at the unit-time coordinate under the chosen policy function. We show that the canonical estimands of the potential outcomes framework — ATE, ATT, ATC, MATE, and CATE — are special cases of TATE corresponding to particular choices of policy function, establishing SPIDER as a strict generalisation of the existing framework. Second, the Variance of Treatment Effects (VTE) quantifies the dispersion of treatment effects under the chosen policy function. We further show that VTE decomposes into components that the treatment assignment mechanism can manipulate and components that it cannot. This decomposition yields a practical lever for risk-sensitive policy design: by adjusting only the treatment assignment mechanism, policymakers can curtail catastrophic treatment effects without switching to a new treatment type or sacrificing the average effect.

SPIDER naturally divides into two parts: (1) SP characterise the distribution of treatment effects at the unit-time coordinates under a prospective policy; and (2) IDER then define the machinery to infer these distributions. This paper develops the first part as a self-contained contribution, which accommodates the main innovations of SPIDER discussed above. First, the S-module models all unit characteristics, time characteristics, and potential outcomes as uncertain quantities generated by the super-population — a departure from the existing potential outcomes framework, which treats these as fixed and takes treatment assignment as the only source of uncertainty. Second, the P-module derives TATE and VTE as policy-induced causal parameters that are important for contemporary policy design and do not exist in the current literature. Beyond their role within SPIDER, the SP-modules are useful as a standalone lens for any empirical research that extrapolates from historical data to prospective policies. For instance, given a study that estimated the canonical ATT, researchers can compare its analytical form with the appropriate TATE to surface the assumptions required for the extrapolation and assess their plausibility. These assumptions often need to be strong, e.g., homogeneous treatment effects across units and time, but are rarely made explicit for scrutiny in current practice.

2.1 Formal Construction of the Measure

The joint random vector ?@eq-sp-randomVector is a measurable function from the underlying probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) to the product space of unit characteristics, time characteristics, and treatment effects:

\[ V^{k,m}_{i,j}: \Omega \rightarrow \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T} \times \mathbb{R} \]

or equivalently,

\[ V^{k,m}_{i,j}(\omega) = (H_i(\omega), T_j(\omega), \Delta^{k,m}_{i,j}(\omega)) \]

Let \(\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}\) denote the pushforward probability measure:

\[ \mu^{k,m}_{i,j} = \mathbb{P} \circ (V^{k,m}_{i,j})^{-1} \]

# Setup {#sec-setup}

We introduce the primitives of the SPIDER framework. This section is
deliberately informal, helping readers understand the building blocks
and context. The probability-theoretic foundations are deferred to the
next sections.

There are four indexing dimensions in the super-population and parameter modules of SPIDER.

- $i \in \mathbb{N}$: Index for individual units (e.g., neighbourhoods, transit stops, or households depending on the application).

- $j \in \mathbb{N}$: Index for discrete time periods (e.g., days, weeks, or months).

- $k \in \{1, \ldots, K\}$: Index for outcomes of interest (e.g., traffic volumes, air pollution levels).

- $m \in \{1, \ldots, M\}$: Index for non-baseline [^1] treatment statuses (e.g., treatment types, intensities). 

[^1]: The baseline treatment status is the absence of treatment.

A distinguishing feature of SPIDER is that the indices $(i,j)$ are not abstract statistical labels but are coordinates of real-world entities: $i$ identifies a specific unit (e.g., the city of London, or   Manchester), and $j$ identifies a specific time period (e.g., the year 2020, 2021). This linkage is deliberate. It encourages researchers to be explicit about which unit-time coordinates their data come from, and how these relate to those targeted by the prospective policy.

The primitive variables are as follows.

- $H_i \in \mathcal{H}$: Characteristics of unit $i$ that do not change over time (e.g., population density, land use mix, proximity to public transport).

- $T_j \in \mathcal{T}$: Characteristics of time period $j$ that do not vary across units (e.g., oil prices, season, day-of-week).

- $Y^{k,0}_{i,j} \in \mathbb{R}$: $k$-th outcome for unit $i$ at time $j$ under the baseline treatment status (i.e., absence of treatment).

- $Y^{k,m}_{i,j} \in \mathbb{R}$: $k$-th outcome for unit $i$ at time $j$ under the non-baseline treatment status $m$.

- $D_{i,j} \in \mathcal{D} = \{d_0, d_1, \ldots, d_M\}$: Treatment status of unit $i$ at time $j$.

We further define the treatment effect of treatment $m$ on outcome $k$ for unit $i$ at time $j$ as follows:

$$
\Delta^{k,m}_{i,j} = Y^{k,m}_{i,j} - Y^{k,0}_{i,j}
$$

The dependency structure of these variables, for each unit-time coordinate $(i,j)$, is
summarised in the following directed acyclic graph (DAG):

```{mermaid}
flowchart LR
    H[H] --> D[D]
    T[T] --> D
    
    H --> Y[Y]
    T --> Y
```

This graph illustrates the dependencies allowed and ruled out by the
SPIDER framework. For example, the unit characteristics $H_i$ can affect
the treatment status $D_{i,j}$ and potential outcome $Y^{k,m}_{i,j}$, but
not the time characteristics $T_j$. SPIDER allows for spatial and temporal dependencies (e.g., $T_{j+1}$ may depend on $T_{j}$), but these relationships cannot be depicted in the unit-time level DAG.

# Super-population {#sec-superpopulation}

The super-population is a stochastic process (or informally a hypothetical data-generating machine) that generates the unit characteristics ($H_i$), time characteristics ($T_j$), and treatment effects ($\Delta^{k,m}_{i,j}$) for all unit-time coordinates:

$$
\mathbf{V} 
= 
\{
  V^{k,m}_{i,j}
\}^{
  1 \leq k \leq K, 1 \leq m \leq M
}_{
  i \geq 1, j \geq 1
}
, \quad \text{where} \quad
V^{k,m}_{i,j} = (H_i, T_j, \Delta^{k,m}_{i,j})
$$ {#eq-sp-randomVector}

## Formalisation

Formally, we define a probability space
$(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ to characterise the output
uncertainty of the super-population. Here: the sample space $\Omega$
represents the set of all possible realisations of the super-population;
the $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}$ is a collection of subsets of
$\Omega$, referred to as events; and the probability measure
$\mathbb{P}$ assigns probabilities to these events.

The variables in $\mathbf{V}$ are measurable functions defined on
this probability space. Each of them maps a realised world
$\omega \in \Omega$ to a value in its state space. For example, 
$\Delta^{k,m}_{i,j}: \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, representing the treatment effect of treatment $m$ on outcome $k$ for unit $i$ at time $j$ in the realised world $\omega$. Under this setup, the randomness in $\Delta^{k,m}_{i,j}$ is induced by the uncertainty on which realised world is generated by the super-population machine [^1].

[^1]: A natural philosophical question arises: if all randomness reflects uncertainty about which world is realised and data collection does not generate a new realised world, then the values of $V^{k,m}_{i,j}$, for any $i,j,k,m$ coordinate, should be invariant across data collections. Yet, in practice, we can observe different values of $V^{k,m}_{i,j}$ across different data collections. For instance, two independent studies measuring traffic volumes in neighbourhood i at day j may report discrepant figures. This discrepancy reflects uncertainties in the measurement process, which is conceptually distinct from the super-population uncertainty modelled here. A natural extension would be to introduce a data collection index $\ell$, replacing $V^{k,m}_{i,j}$ with $V^{k,m}_{i,j,\ell}$, where variation across $\ell$ captures discrepancies arising from different data collections. However, this extension is rarely necessary in practice, particularly in the social sciences, where researchers seldom have multiple data collections for the same (i, j, k, m) coordinate. Therefore, we do not pursue this extension here in the interest of parsimony.

Let $\mu^{k,m}_{i,j} (\cdot)$ denote the induced probability measure of the joint random vector $V^{k,m}_{i,j}$ (this is formally established in @sec-apx:measure). Intuitively, for any measurable set $A \subseteq \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T} \times \mathbb{R}$, the measure $\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(A)$ gives the probability that the random vector $V^{k,m}_{i,j}$ takes a value in $A$ under the super-population.


## Running examples

# Policy Function and Target Measure {#sec-policyDesigns}

Policy design has two aspects. The first is choosing a treatment type or intensity $d_m$ (e.g., a 10% subsidy vs. 20% subsidy). The second is choosing a treatment assignment mechanism. For example, a heating subsidy may be assigned to the household $i$ on day $j$ if their income level and the temperature of the day fall below certain thresholds. The same treatment type $d_m$ applied under different mechanisms can yield different distributions of treatment effects.

SPIDER deliberately excludes treatment assignment from the super-population because it is not a stable feature of the world but rather a variable that policymakers can manipulate. As a result, the measure $\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(\cdot)$ characterising the super-population cannot support policy design on its own. In this section, we introduce a policy-induced target measure that integrates $\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(\cdot)$ with the treatment assignment mechanism. This object is essential for defining the policy-induced parameters in the next section (see @sec-parameters).

To formalise the treatment assignment mechanism, we define a policy function:

$$
\pi^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) 
= 
\mathbb{1} \{(h,t) \in \Pi \} \cdot e^{m}_{i,j}(h,t)
$$

where $\mathcal{\Pi} \subseteq \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T}$ is a measurable set and e^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) is the probability of assigning treatment status $d_m$ to the unit-time coordinate ($i,j$) given characteristics $(h,t)$. We require the policy function to satisfy a regular condition,

$$
0 < \int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T}} \pi^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) \, d\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)
$$

that is, the unit-time coordinate ($i,j$) must have positive probability of receiving treatment $d_m$.

This policy function allows for a wide range of treatment assignment mechanisms. We illustrate two special cases.

- Fixing $e^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) = 1$ for all $(h,t)$, the policy function ($\pi^{m}_{i,j}(h,t)$) denotes a deterministic assignment rule. It assigns treatment status $d_m$ to the unit-time coordinate ($i,j$) if and only if its characteristics $(H_i, T_j)$ fall in $\mathcal{\Pi}$. This naturally represents prospective policy designs, where the policymaker specifies a precise treatment assignment rule.

- Fixing $\mathcal{\Pi} = \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T}$, the policy function ($\pi^{m}_{i,j}(h,t)$) denotes a probabilistic assignment rule. It assigns treatment status $d_m$ to the unit-time coordinate ($i,j$) with a probability that depends on their characteristics $(H_i, T_j)$. This naturally models historical assignment mechanisms, where the exact treatment assignment rule is unknown but assignment probabilities can be estimated from observed data

The original form nests the two special cases and is more general than either. By setting $e^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) < 1$ and $\mathcal{\Pi} \subset \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T}$, simultaneously, the policy function can model a prospective policy design with imperfect delivery considerations. For example, treatment is assigned to the unit-time coordinate ($i,j$) if and only if its characteristics $(H_i, T_j)$ fall in $\mathcal{\Pi}$, but the treatment does not always reach them due to administrative frictions and other implementation barriers. As a result, the unit-time coordinate ($i,j$) receives treatment $d_m$ with a probability that is less than 1, even if its characteristics $(H_i, T_j)$ qualify for treatment.

We define the associated weighting function by normalising the policy function:

$$
w^{m}_{i,j}(h,t)
= 
\frac
  {\pi^{m}_{i,j}(h,t)}
  {\int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T}} \pi^{m}_{i,j}(h',t') \, d\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h',t',\delta)}
$$

Then, we define the policy-induced target measure $\nu^{k,m}_{i,j}$ on $\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T} \times \mathbb{R}$:

$$
\nu^{k,m}_{i,j} (\mathcal{A}; \pi) 
=
\int_{\mathcal{A}} w^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) \, d\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)
$$ {#eq-policy:targetMeasure}

The weighting function $w^{m}{i,j}(h,t)$ serves as the Radon--Nikodym derivative of $\nu^{k,m}{i,j}(\cdot;\pi)$ with respect to $\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(\cdot)$:

$$
\frac{d\nu^{k,m}_{i,j}}{d\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}}(h,t) = w^{m}_{i,j}(h,t)
$$

The weighting function reweights the the super-population towards the environment in which treatment will be deployed. Historical assignment mechanisms (trials, natural experiments, or past policy interventions) may be different from the prospective policies. Therefore, we may need to construct multiple target measures: (1) a target measure based on historical assignment mechanisms for the purpose of learning about the super-population from the data; (2) target measures based on prospective assignment mechanisms to inform prospective policy design. The weighting function provides a unified way to construct all these target measures.

## Running examples {#sec-policy:examples}

We specify five example policy functions: P1, P2, P3, P4, P5. Since we only have one treatment type $d_1$, we suppress the treatment type index $m$ for brevity. The policy functions are as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi^{P1}_{i,j}(h,t) &= 1 \\
\pi^{P2}_{i,j}(h,t) &= \mathbb{1}\{H1_i = 1\} \\
\pi^{P3}_{i,j}(h,t) &= \mathbb{1}\{H1_i = 1, T3_j \geq 0\} \\
\pi^{P4}_{i,j}(h,t) &= \Phi\!\left(2\cdot H1 \right) \\
\pi^{P5}_{i,j}(h,t) &= \mathbb{1}\{T3_j \geq 0\} \cdot \Phi\!\left(2\cdot H1 \right)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\Phi(\cdot)$ denotes the standard normal cumulative distribution function. The first three functions represent deterministic assignment rules; the fourth function represents a probabilistic assignment rule; and the fifth combines a deterministic truncation on $T3_j$ with a probabilistic assignment rule on $H1$.

The weighting functions associated with these policy functions are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w^{P1}_{i,j}(h,t) &= 1 \\
w^{P2}_{i,j}(h,t) &= \frac{\mathbb{1}\{H1_i = 1\}}{0.5} \\
w^{P3}_{i,j}(h,t) &= \frac{\mathbb{1}\{H1_i = 1, T3_j \geq 0\}}{0.5 * (1 - \Phi(-0.01 *(j-1)))} \\
w^{P4}_{i,j}(h,t)
&=
\frac{
\Phi\!\left(2 \cdot h_1\right)
}{
\int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T}}
\Phi\!\left(2 \cdot h'_1\right)
\, d\mu_{i,j}(h',t',\delta)
} \\
w^{P5}_{i,j}(h,t)
&=
\frac{
\mathbb{1}\{t \geq 0\} \cdot \Phi\!\left(2 \cdot h_1\right)
}{
\int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T}}
\mathbb{1}\{t' \geq 0\} \cdot \Phi\!\left(2 \cdot h'_1\right)
\, d\mu_{i,j}(h',t',\delta)
}
\end{aligned}
$$

The denominator in these weighting functions denotes the probability that a unit-time coordinate ($i,j$) receives treatment under the policy function.

We then apply these policy functions to the three super-populations (S1, S2, S3) constructing 15 policy-induced target measures. Since the running examples only have one outcome, the outcome index $k$ is suppressed for brevity. We denote the P1-induced target measure, for super-population S1, as $\nu^{S1,P1}_{i,j}(\cdot)$. The same notation applies to all other super-population and policy combinations.

By substituting the weighting functions into the definition of the policy-induced target measure in @eq-policy:targetMeasure, we can derive the explicit form of each target measure. For illustration, the five policy-induced target measures for for S3 are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nu^{S3,P1}_{i,j}(\mathcal{A})
&=
\int_{\mathcal{A}} d\mu^{S3}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta) = \mu^{S3}_{i,j}(\mathcal{A}) 
\\
\nu^{S3,P2}_{i,j}(\mathcal{A})
&=
\int_{\mathcal{A}} 2 * \mathbb{1}\{H1_i = 1\} \, d\mu^{S3}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)
\\
\nu^{S3,P3}_{i,j}(\mathcal{A})
&=
\int_{\mathcal{A}} \frac{\mathbb{1}\{H1_i = 1, T3_j \geq 0\}}{0.5 (1 - \Phi(-0.01 *(j-1)))} \, d\mu^{S3}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)
\\
\nu^{S3,P4}_{i,j}(\mathcal{A})
&=
\int_{\mathcal{A}}
\frac{
\Phi\!\left(2 \cdot h_1\right)
}{
\int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T}}
\Phi\!\left(2 \cdot h'_1\right)
\, d\mu^{S3}_{i,j}(h',t',\delta)
}
\, d\mu^{S3}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)
\\
\nu^{S3,P5}_{i,j}(\mathcal{A})
&=
\int_{\mathcal{A}}
\frac{
\mathbb{1}\{t \geq 0\} \cdot \Phi\!\left(2 \cdot h_1\right)
}{
\int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T}}
\mathbb{1}\{t' \geq 0\} \cdot \Phi\!\left(2 \cdot h'_1\right)
\, d\mu^{S3}_{i,j}(h',t',\delta)
}
\, d\mu^{S3}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)
\end{aligned}
$$

For each super-population, we plot the marginal distributions of the treatment effect $\Delta_{i,j}$ under the five policy-induced target measures. All the measures under S3 depend on time $j$ through $T3_j \sim N(0.01(j-1), 1)$. Therefore, we must fix a specific time period to plot the distributions of S3. We choose $j=121$ so that $T3_{121} \sim N(1.2, 1)$.

# Helper: numerically integrate out T3 for a given H1 value
# T3 ~ N(pi_121, 1), optionally truncated below at T3_lower
# Delta = intercept + h + T3 + eta, eta ~ N(0, 2)
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
integrate_T3 <- function(delta, intercept, h, sd_eta, T3_lower = -Inf, T3_upper = Inf) {
  integrand <- function(t3) {
    dtruncnorm(t3, a = T3_lower, b = T3_upper, mean = pi_121, sd = 1) *
      dnorm(delta, mean = intercept + h + t3, sd = sd_eta)
  }
  integrate(integrand, lower = T3_lower, upper = T3_upper)$value
}

                            T3_lower = -Inf, T3_upper = Inf,

# Policy-induced Parameters {#sec-parameters}

We define the Target Average Treatment Effect (TATE) as a functional of policy-induced the target measure $\nu^{k,m}_{i,j}$:

$$
TATE^{k,m}_{i,j} 
= 
\int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T} \times \mathbb{R}} 
\delta \,
d\nu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)
$$ {#eq-param:tate}

Intuitively, TATE is the average treatment effect of treatment $m$ on outcome $k$ for the unit-time pair $(i,j)$ in the target environment defined by $\nu^{k,m}_{i,j}$. By varying the policy function $\pi^{m}_{i,j}(h,t)$, we can construct different target measures $\nu^{k,m}_{i,j}$ and therefore different TATEs.

## Special Cases of TATE {#sec-param:special-cases}

TATE is a general class of causal parameters. Many traditional causal parameters in policy evaluation are special cases of TATE, corresponding to different choices of policy functions.

### Average Treatment Effect (ATE) {#sec-param:ate}

Setting the policy function as follows:

$$
\pi^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) = 1
$$

That is, treatment status $d_m$ is assigned to the unit-time pair ($i,j$) regardless of their characteristics. The weighting function is then given by:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) = 1 \ , \quad d \nu^{k,m}_{i,j}(\cdot) = 1 \cdot d\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(\cdot)
\end{aligned}
$$  

Substituting $d \nu^{k,m}_{i,j}(\cdot) = w^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) \, d\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)$ into @eq-param:tate, we have:

$$
TATE^{k,m}_{i,j}
=  \int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T} \times \mathbb{R}} \delta \, d\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta) 
=  \mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta^{k,m}_{i,j}\right]
=  ATE^{k,m}_{i,j}
$$

In other words, the target environment coincides with the super-population itself, i.e., no reweighting is needed. TATE reduces to the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) of treatment $m$ on outcome $k$ for the unit-time pair $(i,j)$:


### Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE) {#sec-param:cate}

Fixing a policy set $\mathcal{\Pi} \subset \{ \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T} \}$ and setting the policy function as follows:

$$
\pi^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) = \mathbb{1}\{(h,t) \in \mathcal{\Pi}\}
$$

That is, treatment status $d_m$ is assigned to the unit-time pair ($i,j$) if and only if its characteristics $(H_i, T_j)$ fall in $\mathcal{\Pi}$. The weighting function is then given by:

$$
w^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) = \frac{\mathbb{1}\{(h,t) \in \mathcal{\Pi}\}}{\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(\mathcal{\Pi} \times \mathbb{R})}
$$

Substituting $d \nu^{k,m}_{i,j}(\cdot) = w^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) \, d\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)$ into @eq-param:tate, we have:

$$
TATE^{k,m}_{i,j}
= \int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T} \times \mathbb{R}} \delta \, w^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) \, d\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta) 
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta^{k,m}_{i,j} \mid (H_i, T_j) \in \mathcal{\Pi}\right]
= CATE^{k,m}_{i,j}(\mathcal{\Pi})
$$

In the target environment, only the treatment effects conditional on $(H_i, T_j) \in \mathcal{\Pi}$ are relevant. More weights are placed on these conditional treatment effects, while no weight is placed on the others. TATE reduces to the Conditional, on $(H_i, T_j) \in \mathcal{\Pi}$, Average Treatment Effect (CATE) of treatment $m$ on outcome $k$ for the unit-time pair $(i,j)$.


### Marginal Average Treatment Effect (MATE)

This is a special case of CATE. Using the same policy function and specifying $\mathcal{\Pi} = \{h\} \times \mathcal{T}$ (i.e., we restrict to a specific value of $H_i$ but not $T_j$), we have:

$$
TATE^{k,m}_{i,j}
= 
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta^{k,m}_{i,j} \mid H_i = h\right] 
= 
MATE^{k,m}_{i,j}(h)
$$

TATE (or CATE) reduces to the Marginal Average Treatment Effect (MATE) of treatment $m$ on outcome $k$ for the unit-time pair $(i,j)$, which is the average treatment effect conditional on a specific value of or set of unit characteristics.

### Average Treatment Effect on the Treated and Control (ATT, ATC) {#sec-param:att}

Setting the policy function as follows:

$$
\pi^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) = e^{m}_{i,j}(h,t) = P(D_{i,j} = d_m \mid H_i = h, T_j = t)
$$

That is, treatment status $d_m$ is assigned to the unit-time pair ($i,j$) with a probability $e^{m}_{i,j}(h,t)$ that depends on their characteristics $(H_i, T_j)$. The weighting function is then given by:

$$
w_{i,j}(h,t) 
= 
\frac{e^{m}_{i,j}(h,t)}
{\int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T}} e^{m}_{i,j}(h',t') \, d\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h',t',\delta)}
=
\frac
  {e^{m}_{i,j}(h,t)}
  {P(D_{i,j} = d_m)}
$$

The denominator is the marginal probability of receiving treatment $d_m$ under the super-population. Substituting $d \nu^{k,m}_{i,j}(\cdot) = w_{i,j}(h,t) \, d\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)$ into @eq-param:tate, we have:

$$
TATE^{k,m}_{i,j}
=
\int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T} \times \mathbb{R}} \delta \, w_{i,j}(h,t) \, d\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)
=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta^{k,m}_{i,j} | D_{i,j} = d_m\right]
=
ATT^{k,m}_{i,j}
$$

In the target environment, all conditional treatment effects are relevant, but more weights are placed on those with higher probabilities of receiving treatment according to the policy function. TATE reduces to the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT) of treatment $m$ on outcome $k$ for the unit-time pair $(i,j)$, which is the average treatment effect conditional on receiving treatment $d_m$. ATC follows analogously by replacing $e^{m}_{i,j}(h,t)$ with $e^{0}_{i,j}(h,t) = P(D_{i,j} = d_0 \mid H_i = h, T_j = t)$, which denotes the probability of receiving control status $d_0$.

## Variance of Treatment Effects {#sec-param:vte}

The variance of treatment effect $\Delta^{k,m}_{i,j}$ is a useful but overlooked parameter in current literature and practice. @sec-policy:examples illustrates that choosing different policy functions (i.e., treatment assignment mechanisms) can change the variability of treatment effects. We formalise this notion in the current section.

We define the variance of treatment effects (VTE) under the policy-induced target measure $\nu^{k,m}_{i,j}$ as:

$$
VTE^{k,m}_{i,j}
= 
\int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T} \times \mathbb{R}} 
\left(\delta - TATE^{k,m}_{i,j}\right)^2 
d\nu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)
$$ {#eq-param:vte}

Then, we can decompose VTE into three components:

$$
VTE^{k,m}_{i,j} 
= 
VTE^{Unit}_{i,j,k,m} +
VTE^{Time|Unit}_{i,j,k,m} + 
VTE^{Resid}_{i,j,k,m}
$$

where 

(1) $VTE^{Unit}_{i,j,k,m}$ captures the variance of treatment effects explained by unit characteristics $H_i$:

$$
VTE^{Unit}_{i,j,k,m}
=
\int_{\mathcal{H}, \mathcal{T}, \mathbb{R}}
\left(MATE^{k,m}_{i,j}(h) - TATE^{k,m}_{i,j}\right)^2
d \nu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)
$$

(2) $VTE^{Time|Unit}_{i,j,k,m}$ captures the variance of treatment effect explained by time characteristics $T_j$ conditional on unit characteristics $H_i$:

$$
VTE^{Time|Unit}_{i,j,k,m}
=
\int_{\mathcal{H}, \mathcal{T}, \mathbb{R}}
  \left(CATE^{k,m}_{i,j} (h,t) - MATE^{k,m}_{i,j}(h)\right)^2
d \mu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h)
$$

(3) $VTE^{Resid}_{i,j,k,m}$ captures the residual variance of treatment effects:

$$
VTE^{Resid}_{i,j,k,m}
=
\int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T}, \mathbb{R}}
  \left(\delta - CATE^{k,m}_{i,j} (h,t)\right)^2
d \nu^{k,m}_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)
$$

Taken together, this decomposition reavels the sources of treatment effect heterogeneity in the target environment. It has significant implications for designing empirical strategies or policy interventions. We discuss these implications in @sec-policyImplications

## Running examples {#sec-parameters:examples}

Recall the 15 policy-induced target measures constructed by combining three super-populations (S1, S2, S3) and five policy functions (P1, P2, P3, P4, P5) in @sec-policy:examples. We can compute the TATE and VTE parameters for each of these 15 combinations.

Since these parameters may vary across the time dimension $j$ in S3, we must fix a specific time period to compute these parameters. The time coordinate $j=121$ is chosen, so that $T3_{121} \sim N(1.2, 1)$. We use Monte Carlo simulation compute these parameters.

  sum(w * x) / sum(w)

  sum(w * (x - mu)^2) / sum(w)
}

# Advantages of TATE and VTE {#sec-policyImplications}

In our running examples, $TATE^{P1}_{i,j}$ reduce to the canonical $ATE_{i,j}$ estimand (see @sec-param:ate). Suppose the prospective treatment assignment mechanism of interest is P2, P3, P4, or P5, through the SPIDER lens, the true average effect is $TATE^{P2}_{i,j}$, $TATE^{P3}_{i,j}$, $TATE^{P4}_{i,j}$, or $TATE^{P5}_{i,j}$, respectively. The numerical results in @tbl-parameters clearly show that TATEs are not generally equivalent to the canonical ATE estimand.

We examine under what conditions the canonical estimand (ATE) can represent the true policy-induced average effect (TATE), and when they may mislead policy design. To generalise the results, we reparameterise the treatment effects of S2 and S3 as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta^{S2}_{i,j} & = -10 + \beta^{H1}_{i,j} H1_i                   + \eta_{i,j} \\
\Delta^{S3}_{i,j} & = -10 + \beta^{H1}_{i,j} H1_i + \beta^{T3}_{i,j} T3_j + \eta_{i,j}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\beta^{H1}_{i,j}$ captures the degree of treatment effect heterogeneity across units, $\beta^{T3}_{i,j}$ captures the degree of treatment effect heterogeneity across time, and $\eta_{i,j} \sim N(0, 2)$ is the error term capturing the residual heterogeneity of treatment effects. In the previous numerical examples, we set $\beta^{H1}_{i,j} = 1$ and $\beta^{T3}_{i,j} = 1$. 

We also reparameterise the policy functions P4 and P5 as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi^{P4}_{i,j} (h,t) & = \Phi(\theta h_1) \\
\pi^{P5}_{i,j} (h,t) & = \mathbb{1}\{t_3 \geq 0\} \cdot \Phi(\theta h_1)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\theta$ captures the degree of targeting on the unit characteristic $H1_i$. In the previous numerical examples, we set $\theta = 2$.

We introduce two objects that compactly summarise how the policy functions transform the means of $H1_i$ and $T3_j$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda^{H1}_{i,j} (\theta) & 
= \frac
  {\Phi(\theta)}
  {0.5 + \Phi(\theta)}
\\
\lambda^{T3}_{i,j} (\varpi_j) &
= \frac
  {\phi(\varpi_j)}
  {\Phi(\varpi_j)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term $\lambda^{H1}_{i,j} (\theta)$ captures the degree of targeting on the unit characteristic $H1_i$ under the probabilistic policy functions P4 and P5. The most intuitive interpretation of this term is that it denotes the probability of $H1_i = 1$ given the unit-time coordinate ($i,j$) receives treatment. 

The second term $\lambda^{T3}_{i,j} (\varpi_j)$ captures the degree of targeting on the time characteristic $T3_j$ under the policy functions P3 and P5. The most intuitive interpretation of this term is that it denotes the increase in the mean of $T3_j$ given the unit-time coordinate ($i,j$) receives treatment, i.e.,:

$$
\lambda^{T3}_{i,j} (\varpi_j) = \mathbb{E}[T3_j \mid T3_j \geq 0] - \mathbb{E}[T3_j]
$$

We introduce an object that compactly summarises how the policy function transforms the variance of $H1_i$:

$$
\kappa^{H1}_{i,j} (\theta) 
= \frac
  {0.5 \Phi(\theta)}
  {\left(0.5 + \Phi(\theta)\right)^2}
$$

This term captures the extent to which the policy function changes the variance of $H1_i$. The most intuitive interpretation of this term is that it denotes the variance of $H1_i$ given the unit-time coordinate ($i,j$) receives treatment. 

Using these objects, we present the analytical forms of TATEs and VTEs for each of the 15 combinations of super-populations and policy functions in @sec-apx:analyticalForm. These are essentially the analytical counterparts of the numerical results in @tbl-parameters [^1]. In the main text, we present the deviation of TATEs under P2, P3, P4, and P5 from TATE under P1, or equivalently the canonical ATE estimand (see @tbl-bias).

[^1]: It is straightforward to verify that the numerical results are consistent with these analytical forms by substituting the parameter values used in the numerical examples into these analytical forms. 

It makes clear that the deviation is governed by the interaction between two objects: (1) the dimensions along which the policy function assigns treatment, and (2) the dimensions along which treatment effects are heterogeneous. Policies P2 and P4 assign treatment based on the unit characteristic $H1_i$, so the deviation of TATEs under P2 and P4 from ATE is governed by the degree of treatment effect heterogeneity across units (i.e., $\beta^{H1}_{i,j}$) and the degree of the policy targeting on $H1_i$ (i.e., $\lambda^{H1}_{i,j}(\theta)$). Policy P3 and P5 assign treatment additionally based on the time characteristic $T3_j$, so the deviation of TATEs under P3 and P5 from ATE is also governed by the degree of treatment effect heterogeneity across time (i.e., $\beta^{T3}_{i,j}$) and the degree of the policy targeting on $T3_j$ (i.e., $\lambda^{T3}_{i,j}(\varpi_j)$).

To formalise the insights from the table, we provide a general condition under which the canonical ATE coincides with the policy-induced TATE in the following theorem.

## Theorem 1: Equivalence of $$ATE$$ and $$TATE$$ for a given policy {#sec-thm-ate-tate}

Let

$$
ATE_{i,j}
=
\int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T} \times \mathbb{R}}
\delta \,
d\mu_{i,j}(h,t,\delta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\Delta_{i,j}\right]
$$

denote the average treatment effect under the super-population measure $$\mu_{i,j}$$, and let

$$
TATE_{i,j}(\pi)
=
\int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T} \times \mathbb{R}}
\delta \,
d\nu_{i,j}(h,t,\delta;\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\nu^\pi}\!\left[\Delta_{i,j}\right]
$$

denote the target average treatment effect induced by policy function $$\pi$$. If

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\Delta_{i,j}\mid \pi(H_i,T_j)\right]
=
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\Delta_{i,j}\right]
\qquad
\mu\text{-almost surely},
$$

then

$$
TATE_{i,j}(\pi)=ATE_{i,j}.
$$

**Proof.** By definition of the policy-induced measure,

$$
TATE_{i,j}(\pi)
=
\int
\delta \, w^\pi_{i,j}(h,t)\, d\mu_{i,j}(h,t,\delta),
$$

where

$$
w^\pi_{i,j}(h,t)
=
\frac{\pi_{i,j}(h,t)}
{\int_{\mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T}} \pi_{i,j}(h',t')\, d\mu_{i,j}(h',t',\delta)}.
$$

Applying iterated expectation conditional on $$\pi(H_i,T_j)$$ yields

$$
TATE_{i,j}(\pi)
=
\int
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\Delta_{i,j}\mid \pi(H_i,T_j)\right]
w^\pi_{i,j}(h,t)\,
d\mu_{H,T}(h,t).
$$

Under the stated condition, the conditional mean treatment effect is constant and equal to $$ATE_{i,j}$$, so

$$
TATE_{i,j}(\pi)
=
ATE_{i,j}
\int
w^\pi_{i,j}(h,t)\, d\mu_{H,T}(h,t)
=
ATE_{i,j},
$$

since $$w^\pi_{i,j}$$ is normalised to integrate to one. $$\square$$

Theorem 1 establishes a sufficient condition for equivalence between the retrospective and prospective estimands. It shows that the canonical $$ATE_{i,j}$$ remains policy-relevant when the conditional mean treatment effect is invariant to the policy function’s assignment rule. Put differently, reweighting the super-population through the policy function leaves the mean treatment effect unchanged when the policy does not systematically favour unit-time coordinates with different expected treatment effects.

The condition in Theorem 1 is policy-specific. In particular, it depends on the argument of the policy function $$\pi(H_i,T_j)$$ rather than on all characteristics jointly. This yields sharper equivalence conditions for different classes of policy functions.

## Corollary 1: Unit-targeting policies {#sec-cor-unit-target}

Suppose the policy function depends only on unit characteristics, so that

$$
\pi_{i,j}(h,t)=\pi_i(h).
$$

If

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\Delta_{i,j}\mid H_i\right]
=
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\Delta_{i,j}\right]
\qquad
\mu\text{-almost surely},
$$

then

$$
TATE_{i,j}(\pi)=ATE_{i,j}.
$$

**Proof.** If $$\pi_{i,j}(h,t)=\pi_i(h)$$, then $$\pi(H_i,T_j)$$ is measurable with respect to $$H_i$$. Mean-independence of $$\Delta_{i,j}$$ from $$H_i$$ therefore implies mean-independence from $$\pi(H_i,T_j)$$. The result follows from Theorem 1. $$\square$$

## Corollary 2: Time-targeting policies {#sec-cor-time-target}

Suppose the policy function depends only on time characteristics, so that

$$
\pi_{i,j}(h,t)=\pi_j(t).
$$

If

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\Delta_{i,j}\mid T_j\right]
=
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\Delta_{i,j}\right]
\qquad
\mu\text{-almost surely},
$$

then

$$
TATE_{i,j}(\pi)=ATE_{i,j}.
$$

**Proof.** The argument is identical to that of Corollary 1, replacing $$H_i$$ with $$T_j$$. $$\square$$

## Corollary 3: Joint unit-time targeting policies {#sec-cor-joint-target}

Suppose the policy function depends jointly on unit and time characteristics. If

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\Delta_{i,j}\mid H_i,T_j\right]
=
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\Delta_{i,j}\right]
\qquad
\mu\text{-almost surely},
$$

then

$$
TATE_{i,j}(\pi)=ATE_{i,j}.
$$

**Proof.** Since $$\pi(H_i,T_j)$$ is measurable with respect to $$\sigma(H_i,T_j)$$, mean-independence of $$\Delta_{i,j}$$ from $$\left(H_i,T_j\right)$$ implies mean-independence from $$\pi(H_i,T_j)$$. The result follows from Theorem 1. $$\square$$

These corollaries clarify that the equivalence condition need not be stated with respect to all characteristics in the super-population. Rather, it must hold only with respect to the characteristics on which the prospective policy reallocates treatment. This distinction is important for policy design. A policy that targets only units through $$H_i$$ is insensitive to treatment-effect heterogeneity across time characteristics $$T_j$$, whereas a policy that targets jointly on $$H_i$$ and $$T_j$$ is not.

We now specialise these results to the running examples.

## Corollary 4: Running examples $$S2$$ and $$S3$$ {#sec-cor-running-examples}

Under

$$
\Delta^{S2}_{i,j}
=
-10 + \beta^{H1}_{i,j} H1_i + \eta_{i,j},
$$

the equivalence

$$
ATE_{i,j}=TATE^{P2}_{i,j}=TATE^{P4}_{i,j}
$$

holds if

$$
\beta^{H1}_{i,j}=0.
$$

Under

$$
\Delta^{S3}_{i,j}
=
-10 + \beta^{H1}_{i,j} H1_i + \beta^{T3}_{i,j} T3_j + \eta_{i,j},
$$

the equivalence

$$
ATE_{i,j}=TATE^{P2}_{i,j}=TATE^{P4}_{i,j}
$$

holds if

$$
\beta^{H1}_{i,j}=0,
$$

while the stronger equivalence

$$
ATE_{i,j}=TATE^{P3}_{i,j}=TATE^{P5}_{i,j}
$$

holds if

$$
\beta^{H1}_{i,j}=0
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
\beta^{T3}_{i,j}=0.
$$

**Proof.** Policy functions $$P2$$ and $$P4$$ target only on $$H1_i$$, so by Corollary 1, equivalence with $$ATE_{i,j}$$ requires homogeneity of the conditional mean treatment effect with respect to $$H1_i$$. In both $$S2$$ and $$S3$$, this is equivalent to $$\beta^{H1}_{i,j}=0$$. Policy functions $$P3$$ and $$P5$$ target jointly on $$H1_i$$ and $$T3_j$$, so by Corollary 3, equivalence further requires homogeneity with respect to $$T3_j$$, which in $$S3$$ is equivalent to $$\beta^{T3}_{i,j}=0$$. $$\square$$

Corollary 4 gives the substantive interpretation of Table @tbl-bias. In $$S2$$, the canonical $$ATE_{i,j}$$ fails to represent the policy-relevant $$TATE_{i,j}$$ whenever the treatment effect varies systematically across units through $$H1_i$$. In $$S3$$, the discrepancy is larger because some policy functions also target across time through $$T3_j$$. The canonical $$ATE_{i,j}$$ is therefore valid for prospective policy design only under a form of treatment-effect homogeneity tailored to the assignment rule under consideration.

[^ate-tate-a.s.]: Here, “$$\mu$$-almost surely” means that the equality holds for all values of the conditioning variables except possibly on a set with probability zero under the super-population measure $$\mu$$.

## Formal Construction of the Measure {#sec-apx:measure}

The joint random vector @eq-sp-randomVector is a measurable function from the underlying probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ to the product space of unit characteristics, time characteristics, and treatment effects:

$$
V^{k,m}_{i,j}: \Omega \rightarrow \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{T} \times \mathbb{R}
$$

or equivalently,

$$
V^{k,m}_{i,j}(\omega) = (H_i(\omega), T_j(\omega), \Delta^{k,m}_{i,j}(\omega))
$$

Let $\mu^{k,m}_{i,j}$ denote the pushforward probability measure:

$$
\mu^{k,m}_{i,j} = \mathbb{P} \circ (V^{k,m}_{i,j})^{-1}
$$
